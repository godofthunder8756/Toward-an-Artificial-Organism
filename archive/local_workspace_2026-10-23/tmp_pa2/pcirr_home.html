Quantifying the impact of age-related hearing loss on neural signatures of target selection and distractor suppression

Nataliya Solmssen, Felix Stockar, Mohsen Alavash, Tzvetan Popov, Malte Wöstmann, Jonas Obleser, Basil C. Preisig https://osf.io/rt6kb/files/osfstorage/6ad0ec5992f6490cef36504c

Neural distractor suppression weakens with age while the influence of hearing loss remains inconclusive

Recommended by Chris Chambers based on reviews by Mareike Daeglau and Benedikt Zoefel
Hearing loss (HL) affects 1 in 5 people worldwide and presents a particularly major challenge for listeners in multi-talker environments due to the need for auditory selective attention. Currently, hearing aids and other assistive technologies are unable to account for the attentional demands of a given environment because the device cannot know which voices or sounds need to be attended (the target) and which must be suppressed (the distractors). For this reason, understanding the neural and behavioural correlates of selective attention in HL, compared to the case of normal hearing (NH), is crucial for a complete understanding of HL and for developing attention-led rehabilitation strategies.
 
Previous research has shown that individuals with HL reliably demonstrate attentional impairments and that these deficits are reflected in the lateralisation of alpha-band oscillatory activity (8–12 Hz) recorded with electroencephalography (EEG). Alpha lateralisation is thus assumed to reflect (at least partially) the activity of an attentional filtering system, but due to design limitations of previous work, it has remained unclear whether this signature applies to both target selection and distractor suppression.
 
Here, Solmssen et al. (2026) addressed this evidentiary gap using EEG and an established pitch discrimination task that separates target selection from distractor suppression (Wöstmann et al., 2019). First, they asked whether HL (vs NH) was reflected in impaired neural signatures (alpha lateralisation) of target selection and distractor suppression; second, they tested whether changes in neural target selection and distractor suppression in individuals with HL predicted listening performance (thus furnishing a direct link between brain activity and behaviour); and finally, they asked whether changes in attentional control – as measured with alpha lateralisation – were reflected by changes in horizontal eye movements (microsaccades).
 
The results provided inconclusive evidence as to whether mild-to-moderate HL affects target- or distractor-related alpha lateralisation. By contrast, distractor-related, but not target-related, alpha lateralisation diminished with age. At the single-trial level, alpha lateralisation did not predict either listening accuracy or subjective listening confidence. Finally, microsaccade rate was increased during distractor suppression compared with target selection, but microsaccade direction did not mirror the pattern of neural alpha lateralisation. Taken together, the findings suggest that ageing more strongly affects the neural signature of distractor suppression than target selection, while the evidence for an additional impairment specifically attributable to hearing loss remains inconclusive.
 
The Stage 2 manuscript was evaluated over one round of in-depth review by two reviewers. Following detailed revision and response, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 2 criteria and awarded a positive recommendation.

Neural Responses to Psychological and Physical Pain in Social Anxiety: An fMRI Study

I. Chałatkiewicz, A. Duszyk-Bogorodzka, K. Jankowiak-Siuda https://osf.io/9se5g/files/brfau?view_only=3d968c613915459a82d88201a2707e21

How is social anxiety related to perceiving physical and psychological pain in others?

Recommended by Helena Hartmann based on reviews by Matthias Sperl, Barbara Schmidt and 1 anonymous reviewer
Empathy for others' pain involves both understanding another person's experience and experiencing an emotional response to their suffering. However, it remains unclear how individual differences in social anxiety shape these responses, particularly when observing psychologically painful interpersonal situations rather than physical injury.
 
In this study, Chałatkiewicz et al. (2026) will conduct an experimental fMRI study to better understand the relationship between high vs. low social anxiety and cognitive vs. affective empathic responses. These responses can also be categorized into other- vs. self-focused, respectively, as has often been done in previous empathy work.
 
An advantage of the present study is that the authors plan to include stimuli depicting different forms of psychological pain (as opposed to limited stimuli or specific psychological pain in the past). Moreover, they will be able to disentangle the relationship of social anxiety with cognitive/other-focused vs. affective/self-focused forms of empathy. Finally, a table clearly delineates research questions, hypotheses, sampling and analysis plans as well as interpretations based on different outcomes.
 
The Stage 1 manuscript was evaluated over two rounds of review by three expert reviewers. Following the authors' detailed revision and response, the recommender judged the manuscript to meet the Stage 1 criteria and awarded in-principle acceptance.

Does the valence-dominance model apply to deepfake faces? Validation and its impact on deepfake detection

Gianmarco Convertino, Mara Stockner, Michela Marchetti, Danilo Mitaritonna, Luca Corvitto, Luca Maiano, Lorenzo Papa, Irene Amerini, Giuliana Mazzoni https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/7rqps_v2

Testing the limits of the valence-dominance model in deepfake face perception

Recommended by Chris Chambers based on reviews by Yuki Yamada
According to the popular valence-dominance model of face perception, social judgments of faces are defined along two dimensions: valence (trustworthiness) and dominance (perceived physical strength and capacity to pose a threat). Here, Convertino et al. (2026) tested whether this model also characterises how people perceive deepfake faces generated using AI-driven diffusion technology, and examined how social evaluations of faces are associated with people’s ability to distinguish deepfake from real faces.
 
The results provide a nuanced answer. The valence-dominance structure was successfully reproduced for real faces and was also evident in exploratory analyses of faces that participants subjectively categorised as deepfake. For actual deepfake faces, however, low reliability of several attributes, including trustworthiness, prevented the primary hypothesis from being fully evaluated according to the registered criteria; the resulting reduced analysis was therefore inconclusive. In analyses of detection performance, dominance was associated with greater accuracy, while exploratory analyses identified associations involving several individual social attributes. Sensitivity analyses that excluded attributes with low reliability provided the clearest evidence for an association involving caringness.
 
The study provides a detailed and informative test of whether a well-established model of human face perception generalises to highly realistic AI-generated faces, while also highlighting important limits on the reliability and interpretation of social evaluations in this context. The findings are relevant not only to theories of face perception but also to applied settings in which people must distinguish authentic from artificially generated facial images.
 
Following in-depth review and revision, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 2 criteria and awarded a positive recommendation.

Mapping Auditory Neuroplasticity: A Registered Report Systematic Review of Speech Perception Interventions

Karlien De Temmerman, Marta Topor, Erik Marsja, Leire Martin, Carine Signoret https://osf.io/yjku9/files/w4mkd

Understanding the neural effects of interventions to improve speech perception in noise

Recommended by Stefano Coretta based on reviews by Jared Carter and Katrin Krumbholz
Understanding speech in noisy environments is a challenging process, particularly for individuals with hearing loss. Various interventions, including auditory, musical and cognitive training, have been developed to improve speech perception under these conditions. However, their behavioural benefits have been variable, and relatively few studies have examined accompanying changes in neural function.
 
In this Stage 1 submission, De Temmerman et al. (2026) propose a systematic review of neurophysiological changes associated with interventions designed to improve speech perception in noise. The review aims to address an important gap in the literature by synthesising evidence across neuroimaging modalities, intervention types, and populations, while also considering whether the timing of post-intervention assessments influences observed neural and behavioural effects. The authors will use a systematic search and screening procedure, followed by a descriptive narrative synthesis examining patterns of neuroplasticity, the transfer of behavioural improvements beyond trained tasks, and the methodological quality of the available evidence.
 
Of particular note, this Registered Report takes the form of a descriptive and exploratory qualitative review, rather than a conventional hypothesis-testing study. As such, it contributes not only to the substantive literature on auditory neuroplasticity but also to the broader development of Registered Reports by providing an informative example of their application in a non-confirmatory research context.
 
The Stage 1 manuscript was evaluated over two rounds of review by two expert reviewers. Following detailed responses to the comments, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 1 criteria and awarded in-principle acceptance.

Characterizing the influence of multiband acceleration and head coil on BOLD signal in the amygdala

Lavinia Wuensch, Jonathan Wirsich, Yoann Stussi, Ben Meuleman, Sylvain Delplanque, Frédéric Grouiller, David Sander, Eva R. Pool https://osf.io/jzu3n/files/u3csn?view_only=8197c892409b46daa2843608ea572a93

How do multiband acceleration and head coil selection influence fMRI of the amygdala?

Recommended by Oscar Esteban based on reviews by Logan Dowdle and 2 anonymous reviewers
The amygdala plays a central role in emotional processing and learning, but its location deep within the brain makes it particularly challenging to image using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Although multiband acceleration offers increased temporal resolution, it can also reduce signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), raising questions about how these trade-offs depend on the choice of receiver coil.
 
The current study by Wuensch et al. (2026) aims to determine whether and how multiband acceleration influences the quality of blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal in the amygdala, using both task-related and task-agnostic measures. They hypothesize that the number and geometric layout of channels in head coils influence the extent to which distance from the receiver coil reduces SNR, potentially driving interactions between the effects of multiband acceleration and receiver coil selection. To investigate these effects, the authors will compare four scanning protocols combining 32- or 64-channel head coils with and without multiband acceleration, using univariate and multivariate analyses of task-related BOLD responses alongside measures of temporal SNR. Such characterization work is critical for informing future research and laying a solid foundation for studying the amygdala with fMRI.
 
Despite the increased difficulty of defining actionable hypotheses in characterization work with substantial methodological components, as in the report at hand, all reviewers agreed on the scientific relevance of the study and the well-designed approach of the proposed experiments. The authors were fully responsive to the feedback they received. On this basis, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 1 criteria and awarded in-principle acceptance.

Imprints of Body-Movement Timing onto Subsequent Processing of Musical Rhythm

Ségolène M. R. Guérin, Emmanuel Coulon, Tomas Lenc, Rainer Polak, Peter E. Keller, Sylvie Nozaradan https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23104572

Movement primes rhythmic behaviour but not neural metre representations

Recommended by Juan David Leongómez based on reviews by Jonathan Cannon and 1 anonymous reviewer
Does moving to a rhythm affect how we hear it later? This question involves motor cognition, enculturation, and neural plasticity, and has been surprisingly difficult to obtain clear and direct experimental evidence to answer it. In the first of two planned Stage 2 articles from a programmatic Stage 1 registered report, Guérin and colleagues (2026) offer a partial but theoretically important answer.
 
Participants with African cultural backgrounds were recorded using EEG and hand-clapping before and after a brief session in which they clapped and stepped to either a three-beat or four-beat interpretation of a West/Central African bell pattern. The results show a clear dissociation: behaviourally, participants’ clapping aligned more closely with the primed metre after the movement session, showing that even a short motor engagement can affect rhythmic behaviour independently of long-term cultural experience. Neurally, however, EEG frequency tagging did not show a similar strengthening of the brain representation of the primed metre. 
 
This difference is the most important contribution of the paper. It raises fundamental questions about whether movement can influence behaviour without creating a lasting change in the brain’s representation of metre, and whether EEG frequency tagging was sensitive enough to detect that change. It remains unclear whether the null EEG result reflects a true absence of neural imprinting or simply a limitation of current EEG methods. A companion study (Coulon et al., in preparation) will investigate this further comparing participants from different cultural backgrounds.
 
The manuscript was evaluated over three rounds of review by two reviewers, who confirmed that the study was conducted in adherence to the preregistered protocol and that the conclusions are justified. On this basis the recommender judged that the manuscript meet the Stage 2 criteria and awarded a positive recommendation.

Positive end-expiratory pressure versus sham valve/zero end-expiratory pressure in cardiopulmonary resuscitation to improve neurological outcomes – the REVIVE-PEEP triple-blind randomized controlled superiority clinical trial in the ARREST registry

Jeroen van Eijk, Patrick Schober, Hans van Schuppen, Judith ter Schure https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.27.26361533

Testing the effectiveness of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) on neurological status in adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest - The REVIVE-PEEP Trial

Recommended by Anna Porter based on reviews by Nicolas Segond and David Purkarthofer
Ventilation for oxygenation is a component of effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) in cardiac arrest patients, both for revival and neurological outcomes (van Eijk et al., 2024). However, there are potential risks to the patient from improper ventilation technique, including hypoxemia (Algahtani, Scott, & Li, 2024). Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is a method of mechanical ventilation for oxygenation used in clinical practice, and has been indicated as a possible method for ventillation during CPR (Tangpaisarn et al., 2023). PEEP has not been vigorously tested for effectiveness in out-of-hospital CPR in randomized controlled trials.
 
In the Resuscitation and Ventilation Innovations Via Evidence-based Positive End Expiratory Pressure (REVIVE-PEEP) trial, van Eijk and colleagues (2026) aim to evaluate whether the application of 8 cm H2O of PEEP, compared to a sham valve applying zero end-expiratory pressure (ZEEP), during manual ventilation after advanced airway management in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest improves neurological outcomes at discharge. The authors will conduct a pragmatic, registry-based, multicenter, parallel-group, triple-blind 1:1 individually randomized controlled superiority clinical trial conducted within the ARREST (AmsteRdam RESustitation STudies) registry, which is comprised of five emergency medical services in the Netherlands. Approximately 3,200 patients aged 18 years and older with non-traumatic out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, after advanced airway management, will be randomized to one of two treatment groups, PEEP or ZEEP, to be delivered by ambulance personnel trained on study procedures. The primary outcome of interest is neurological status at hospital discharge. Several secondary outcomes are also of interest, including any return of spontaneous circulation during the CPR attempt, 30-day survival, favorable neurological outcome, and health-related quality of life at 6 months among survivors.
 
The Stage 1 manuscript was evaluated over three rounds of review by up to two experts who provided extensive comments and suggestions. Based on the authors’ thorough revisions and detailed responses, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 1 criteria and therefore awarded in-principle acceptance.

Using Intensive Longitudinal Methods with children: Scope, methodology, feasibility

Recommended by Marina Bazhydai based on reviews by Juda Kaleta, Mathilde Prenevost and 1 anonymous reviewer
Lo and colleagues (2026) propose to conduct a scoping review of intensive longitudinal methods increasingly used with primary school children, such as experience-sampling, ecological momentary assessment, ambulatory assessment, and daily diaries. With rapid technological advancements, these methods allow for dynamic modelling of the developmental and momentary change to fully embrace the complexity of individual trajectories. Arguably, these methods also help address the crisis in methodological ecological validity. The proposed review will focus specifically on school aged children, which will be particularly valuable to the fields of developmental psychology and educational sciences. The review aims to  address the following questions: 1) what areas of development have been investigated with such methods since 2023; 2) how have these methods been employed; 3) are these methods feasible?

Examining the Relations Between Self-Construal, Culture, and Cognitive Dissonance Using the Induced Compliance Paradigm

Tim Figureau, Daniel Priolo, Willem W. Sleegers, Kenneth G. DeMarree, Omid Ghasemi, Amélie Gourdon-Kanhukamwe, Maxime Mauduy, Asil A. Özdoğru, Robert M. Ross, Bjørn Sætrevik, Kathleen Schmidt, Nicolas Sommet, Royce Anders, Armand Chatard, Dmitrii Dubrov, Thomas R. Evans, Fabien Girandola, Dmitry Grigoryev, Sara Jaubert, Marie-Amélie Martinie, Eva Moreno-Bella, Fatih Sonmez, Florian van Leeuwen, Luc Vieira, Guillermo B. Willis, Marzie Yaghoobnezhad, David C. Vaidis https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/9u7ns_v3

Induced attitude change by writing a counter-attitudinal essay does not depend on an independent versus interdependent construal of the self at the individual or country level

Recommended by Zoltan Dienes based on reviews by Chris Chambers and Peter Smith
One of the key research programmes in social psychology since the 1950s has been cognitive dissonance: The core idea is that if people are induced to have inconsistent cognitions, an aversive state of dissonance is created that is resolved by revising one of the cognitions. This is generally coupled with the claim that behaviour can be influenced by social context in more powerful ways than people believe, so cognitions need to be altered to match socially induced behaviour. For example, in the induced compliance paradigm, people write essays supporting a point of view they have previously said they oppose (e.g. the view that tuition fees should increase for university). They could be simply told to write the essay (low choice) or have consent taken first (high choice), or write on a neutral topic. In the high choice condition the social demands are still to write the essay despite the felt presence of choice, so participants are reliably induced to write the essay. Thus, the argument goes, the person needs to explain the inconsistency between their advocating higher fees and their beliefs, an inconsistency which is high in the high choice condition, where, they believe, they could have avoided advocating for high fees. When opinion on the topic is measured, a classic result is that attitude changes more in the high rather than low choice condition (Croyle & Cooper, 1983).

How does the induced compliance effect fare when replicated as a large scale registered report? Vaidis et al. (2024) replicated the paradigm in 39 labs in 19 countries, with almost 5,000 participants, and found a non-significant difference between high and low choice conditions in attitude change. (One feels a yearning for other dissonance effects to be subjected to such rigorous replication). They did find that people in both conditions changed their attitude more than in a control condition where participants wrote on a neutral topic, that is, one which involved no inconsistency with the target belief. This result is also predicted by dissonance theory. If the manipulation of choice was not strong enough, it would make little difference; but writing a counter-attitudinal essay versus not would still create dissonance and hence attitude change. It is not the only explanation; people may, for example, simply persuade themselves more when writing a counter-attitudinal versus neutral essay by considering good arguments against their original views. If the effect is due to dissonance reduction then it should be sensitive to variables that determine how strong a sense of dissonance is produced by the manipulation.
 
Figureau et al. (2026) conducted secondary analyses on the data of Vaidis et al. to determine individual and culture level differences that should predict individual differences in dissonance reduction. Specifically, if the change had been based on cognitive dissonance, it would have been expected that  independence would have predicted more attitude change than interdependence, because an independent rather than interdependent person would regard their decision to write a counter-attitudinal essay as based on internal versus external attributes. A sense of dissonance should be enhanced if the behaviour is internally rather than externally determined. But Figureau et al. found that the counter-attitudinal versus neutral effect was not predicted by individual level self-construal. In a similar way, if dissonance was operating, attitude change should be predicted by country-level individualism and aggregated self-construal scores. But it was not. In sum, either cognitive dissonance was not involved, or the way it operates needs rethinking. Never before has the theory of a relationship between self-construal in different cultures and dissonance been tested across so many different countries.
 
The Stage 2 manuscript was evaluated over one round of review by two experts who provided extensive comments and suggestions. Based on the authors’ thorough revisions and detailed responses, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 2 criteria awarded a positive recommendation.
