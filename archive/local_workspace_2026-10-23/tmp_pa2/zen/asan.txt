 Decodable, Compositional Affect-Like Representations
in a Self-Taught Agent and Pretrained Language Models

                                                  Abstract
     We study whether emotion-like internal variables arise without hand-coding in artificial net-
     works, and whether affect-related representations share common geometric structure across sys-
     tems. In a self-taught homeostatic reinforcement-learning agent—trained only with a well-being
     reward and no emotion labels or objectives—affect-related information forms a decodable, com-
     positional, low-dimensional subspace. When interoceptive variables are hidden from the input,
     externally grounded affect-like states are still internally constructed. Unsupervised analysis re-
     covers a dominant valence axis together with distinct threat and novelty axes. This geometry is
     largely generic to structured latent states; what survival makes specific is the valuation of those
     states. Hidden-level causal control is weak but specific, while interventions at the interocep-
     tive level produce stronger behavioural effects. In pretrained language models, affect directions
     show moderate alignment with human valence judgments across 1,500 words held out from probe
     training (Pearson r = 0.65, 95% CI [0.62, 0.68]) and respond to targeted activation-steering in-
     terventions along the valence and arousal directions. Phenomenal experience is neither claimed
     nor measurable. Negative results—including a refuted viability-gradient hypothesis and weak
     hidden-level causal control—are reported explicitly.


1 Introduction

Do machines have anything worth calling emotion? We separate two questions that are routinely
conflated. The phenomenal question—whether a system feels—is, given the hard problem of con-
sciousness and the other-minds problem, not decidable by any measurement, and we do not claim
to settle it. The functional question—whether a system computes internal variables that play the
causal and representational roles emotions play—is empirical. This paper is about the functional
question, pursued with controls stringent enough that a sceptic can check each claim, and honest
enough to report the several claims that failed.
    Our stance is constructive rather than promotional. We train an agent to survive, never men-
tioning emotion, and ask what its neurons come to represent; we run the identical read-out on real
language models; and we subject every positive result to a shuffled or matched-random control, a
permutation test, and multiple seeds. Where a result survives, we state it plainly; where it does
not, we say so.

2 Related Work

Our method rests on the linear representation hypothesis and on representation engineering / ac-
tivation steering, which show that high-level features are often linear directions that can be read

and causally added (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023). Our theoretical vocabulary—valence
and arousal as the two axes of core affect, organised as a circumplex—comes from affective science
(Russell, 1980; Barrett, 2017; Panksepp, 1998). The idea that affect is a bodily, viability-tracking
signal draws on interoception, homeostasis and active inference (Damasio, 1996; Seth, 2013; Fris-
ton, 2010), and the constructionist view that emotions are distributed constructions rather than
localised modules (Barrett, 2017). On the machine side we build on homeostatic reinforcement
learning (Keramati and Gutkin, 2011) and on recent discussion of affect and welfare indicators in
AI systems (Butlin et al., 2023).

3 Methods

World and body. A 9×9 grid hosts an agent with a vector of homeostatic needs (hunger, fatigue;
a threat-driven “safety” need; a social “bond” need). Objects reduce needs on contact; a predator
raises the safety deficit when near; a spatial caregiver relieves needs and the bond when reached and
can be permanently lost mid-episode (bereavement); a rival blocks the needed resource (frustration);
a novel tile carries an unknown payoff (curiosity). Well-being is W = 1−maxi diwi over need deficits
di; the per-step reward is W with a terminal penalty for death. No emotion-specific reward term or
auxiliary objective is used—the death penalty is part of the survival signal, not a separate shaping
term. Survival needs are lethal and drive arousal; the social need depresses well-being without being
lethal.

Self-taught learning. By self-taught we mean the absence of emotion-specific supervision—no
emotion labels, no emotion reward term, no emotion-specific objective (the environment’s homeo-
static structure is, of course, designed by us). The agent is trained end-to-end with PPO (Schulman
et al., 2017) from the well-being reward—no behaviour cloning, no reward shaping, no emotion sig-
nal. Difficulty ramps over a developmental curriculum (a world that hardens as the agent matures);
a single network carries its weights through the ramp. All agent results use three independent seeds.

Read-out and manipulation. From frozen agents we fit ridge probes (α=20, five-fold cross-
validated) mapping the penultimate hidden state ht to each interoceptive cause, giving an emotion
direction we and an affect matrix B = [wfear; . . . ; wcomfort]. We test compositionality by moving
fearful states along −wfear and reading decoded well-being against a matched-random move. We
test causal use by activation steering (adding αwe to ht) and by lesioning the affect subspace against
a random subspace. For LLMs (GPT-2 small/medium/large; Pythia-70M/160M/410M/1.4B) we
build a four-quadrant valence×arousal sentence set: 48 affect words (12 per quadrant) crossed with 8
templates give 384 sentences, split by word so that one quarter of the words (12; three per quadrant)
and all of their sentences are held out—no lemma appears in both train and test, so the reported
figures are on unseen words. We mean-pool each layer’s token activations and standardise on the
training split. Decodability is a logistic probe’s binary valence accuracy on the ≈96 held-out-word
test sentences (this is the “99–100%”), against a shuffled-label control; a ridge probe (α=20) gives
continuous scores for the human-norm correlations. Steering is applied at a fixed mid-depth block
(round(0.55 L)); the direction is the training class-mean difference and the coefficients are fixed a
priori (α ∈ {−6, −3, 0, 3, 6}, never tuned on the read-out); we report the change in positive−negative
(and high−low arousal) next-word logits over 15 neutral prompts, against a norm-matched random
direction. Human-norm validation uses 1,500 words randomly sampled from the Warriner et al.
lexicon, disjoint from the probe’s training words.

Controls. Every decodability figure is paired with a shuffled-label control (≈ 0) and, for the

4 Results

4.1 Emergent, Decodable Affect in the Agent
A network trained only to survive develops a hidden state from which each interoceptive/affective
variable is linearly decodable well above chance (Table 1). Across three seeds every emotion beats a
shuffled-label control. With 10,000 permutations (representative seed) each observed R2 exceeds all
shuffles (p ≈ 1 × 10−4), below the Bonferroni threshold 0.05/21 = 0.0024 for the 21 emotion×seed
tests. Crucially, episode-level bootstrap 95% CIs—resampling whole trajectories (120 episodes),
so the effective sample size is episodes, not the ∼22,000 correlated states—remain well above zero
for every emotion (e.g. fear [0.61, 0.80], grief [0.76, 0.93], comfort [0.96, 0.98]; anger, the weakest,
[0.09, 0.34]). The reported R2 are themselves effect sizes. Fear, grief and comfort are the most
cleanly represented; anger is consistently the weakest.

4.2 Affect Is Constructed, Not Merely Copied
A key objection is that decodability could be trivial: the need values are in the observation, so any
competent agent encodes them. We remove this confound by hiding the interoceptive needs from
the input entirely—the agent sees only the last eight external frames (positions, predator, objects,
caregiver, rival, novelty) and must infer its internal state from behavioural history (a frame-stacked
MLP). It still learns to survive (152/200 steps). Crucially, the never-observed affective states that
are inferable from external cues remain decodable from the hidden layer—comfort R2 = 0.90,
curiosity 0.57, grief 0.50, fear 0.42, anger 0.33—whereas the slowly-accumulating drives that would
require counting elapsed time do not (hunger 0.06, fatigue 0.08). The agent therefore constructs
an internal model of exactly the affect it can infer, and only that. For these externally-grounded
emotions the stronger reading is earned: the representation is built, not copied, and cannot be
dismissed as input pass-through.

4.3 Unsupervised Discovery of Affective Axes
Probing for pre-named variables risks circularity—we look for “fear” and find it. We therefore also
decompose the hidden state with no labels at all (PCA and ICA (Hyvärinen and Oja, 2000)) and
only afterwards ask what each axis correlates with. A genuinely emergent organisation appears.
Across all three seeds the dominant principal component (explaining ≈ 50% of the variance) maps


                                                  3
almost perfectly onto reward expectation / well-being (|r| = 0.99)—a valence axis the network was
never asked to build. Independent components then separate distinct environmental factors we
never defined as targets: threat (|r| ≈ 0.5–0.7), novelty (0.6–0.7) and depletion (hunger, fatigue)
each claim their own axis in every seed. The affective structure is thus discovered, not imposed:
a dominant valence dimension with distinct threat and novelty axes falls out of an unlabelled
decomposition, mirroring the primacy of valence in human core affect (Russell, 1980). Notably, the
more conditional social emotions (grief, frustration) do not form their own dominant unsupervised
axis—consistent with their weaker supervised decodability (anger R2 ≈ 0.22) and suggesting that
an affect-like axis emerges in proportion to how strongly and how often the corresponding factor
drives the dynamics.

4.4 The Affect Space Is Compositional
The emotion directions are not a mere bag of features: the subspace supports arithmetic. Moving
fearful states along −wfear raises decoded well-being far more than a matched-random move, in
every seed (mean along-fear 1.0 vs. along-random 0.4; robust 3/3). This is the machine analogue of
word-vector arithmetic (Mikolov et al., 2013) (“fear − threat + safety ≈ relief”).

4.5 Robust to Architecture
The finding is not a single-width artefact: emotions remain decodable across hidden widths 64/128/256
(fear 0.61–0.70, grief 0.80–0.87, comfort 0.91–0.94, curiosity 0.74–0.94), with anger the one persis-
tently weak axis (0.16–0.29).

4.6 Language Models: Causal, Human-Aligned, Geometric Affect
Applying the same read-out framework to pretrained language models reveals several convergent
affect-related findings. Held-out valence is linearly classifiable at 99–100% across scales (shuffled
control ≈ 0.4); we note this rests on only 12 held-out words (three per quadrant) presented through
8 templates, so it reflects a small number of independent lexical items—the 1,500-word benchmark
below is the stronger external test. Steering a mid-depth layer along the valence direction shifts
the model’s own next-word choice monotonically and beats a matched-random direction; the effect
strengthens with scale (Pythia-410M: valence gap −3.7 → +5.8). Arousal is independently steerable
(−5.4 → +0.5 at 410M, random flat): interventions along the learned valence and arousal directions
produce corresponding, direction-specific output shifts that a norm-matched random direction does
not. The two axes orthogonalise with scale: the valence–arousal direction cosine falls from ≈ 0.85 in
small models to ≈ 0 in larger ones in both families (Pythia +0.89 →+0.01 over 70M–410M; GPT-2
−0.83 → +0.06 over 124M–774M), so the valence–arousal orthogonalisation with scale replicates
across two independent architecture families, consistent with the circumplex geometry (we report
a scale-associated trend, not a reproduction of the full model). Finally, external validity against
real human norms. A probe trained only on affect-naming templates, evaluated zero-shot on 1,500
words held out from probe training (randomly sampled from the Warriner et al. lexicon (Warriner
et al., 2013)), recovers their human valence at Pearson r = 0.65 (95% CI [0.62, 0.68]; Spearman
ρ = 0.62) and their arousal at r = 0.31 [0.26, 0.36]—valence aligning far better than arousal, as it
does in the human data. (A small curated set of clearly-valenced words reaches r=0.91, but that
over-states the effect; the large held-out benchmark is the honest figure.) The near-perfect held-out
classification (99–100%, reported above) and this moderate regression correlation are consistent
rather than contradictory: separating the two valence poles is a far easier task than predicting
a graded 1–9 human rating across the many mid-valence words. Applied zero-shot to situational

sentences containing no affect vocabulary (“the results came back and it had spread”), the same
probe still recovers valence at 79% accuracy (24 sentences, 12 positive / 12 negative; chance 50%)—
consistent with an affect-related state representation beyond affect-word semantics, though on few
items and not a full control for it.

4.7 Valence and Reward-Prediction Error
As a supporting analysis (not a central claim), measured valence (∆W ) is modestly correlated with
the critic’s temporal-difference reward-prediction error r + γV (s′) − V (s) at r ≈ 0.20 across all three
seeds (shuffled ≈ 0)—a weak but consistent dopamine-like link (Schultz et al., 1997). Because the
agent’s reward is itself defined from well-being, this relationship is partly built in rather than an
independent discovery; we report it only as convergent with the reward-prediction-error account of
valence.

4.8 Valuation of Affect Is Survival-Specific
Raw decodability is shared between agents (see the negative results below), but valuing the home-
ostatic state is not. For the survival agent the critic assigns higher value to healthier states (Pear-
son corr(V, W ) = +0.60, +0.66, +0.68 across seeds) and valence tracks the reward-prediction error
(corr(∆W, RPE) ≈ +0.19); for a navigation agent with identical senses but needs irrelevant to its
reward, both collapse or invert (corr(V, W ) = −0.71—it will run itself down to reach targets—and
corr(∆W, RPE) = −0.01). Thus the evaluation of affect—treating well-being as reward and letting
mood follow surprise—emerges only under survival pressure, even where the raw representation does
not.

4.9 Affect Variance Is Concentrated in a Low-Dimensional Subspace
The hidden activity is concentrated in very few directions: across seeds the participation ratio is
≈ 1.4 and the effective rank ≈ 2.3 (of 128 units), and three principal components capture 90% of
the variance. The seven emotion directions themselves span ≈ 6.1 effective axes (singular values of
B), i.e. they are largely distinct rather than collapsed onto one another. The apparent tension - an
effective rank of ≈ 2 yet seven directions spanning ≈ 6 axes - dissolves once one notes that the two
quantities measure different things: the effective rank weights directions by variance, and the vari-
ance is dominated by the single valence/reward-expectation axis (the dominant principal component,
≈ 50%; §“Unsupervised Discovery”), whereas the individual emotion directions are near-orthogonal
but sit mostly in the low-variance remainder - decodable, yet not where the representational en-
ergy lives. “Low-dimensional” therefore means the affective variance is concentrated on very few
(chiefly valence) axes, not that the seven emotions are redundant. Three quantities should be kept
distinct: the representation variance is ∼2-dimensional (effective rank), the dominant affective axis
is ∼1-dimensional (valence), and the emotion-direction span is ∼6-dimensional - different measures
of different things, not a contradiction.

4.10 Affect vs. a Generic Latent State
Is this geometry specific to affect, or a generic property of any structured latent state an RL agent
must estimate? We run the identical pipeline on an affect-neutral agent—same architecture and
world, but rewarded for reaching an observable moving target, with needs irrelevant and no death.
It solves its task (reaching the target ∼160 times per episode), and its task-relevant latents are
as cleanly decodable as the survival agent’s affect variables (target distance R2 = 0.91, target

offset 0.88); it too has a dominant, value-correlated principal component. So linear decodability
and a dominant value axis are generic, not affect-specific—exactly the caution a probing result
deserves. One thing differs sharply: the homeostatic agent’s representation is far lower-dimensional
(participation ratio ≈ 1.4, effective rank ≈ 2.3) than the navigator’s (4.8 and 10.0)—homeostatic
regulation compresses to a near-scalar valence axis where navigation spreads across many. We do
not over-read this, as it may reflect the lower intrinsic dimensionality of homeostatic control rather
than affect as such. The honest conclusion is that the geometry—decodability, compositionality,
a value-dominated axis—is largely a property of structured latent-state representation in general;
what our other experiments isolate as genuinely affect-/survival-specific is the valuation of the state
and, in language models, its human alignment, not the geometry itself.

4.11 Causal Influence of Internal Affect
Does the internal affect state drive behaviour or merely accompany it? Two interventions on a frozen
agent, over N =22,308 predator-relevant states from 120 episodes, with bootstrap CIs that resample
episodes. Input level : holding the external world fixed and flipping only the internal affect input
(safety deficit 0 → 0.8) changes the greedy action in 24% of states (95% CI [23, 26]), toward fleeing
in the adaptive cases (7% [6, 7]). Hidden level : activation patching of the affect subspace induces
adaptive fleeing in 9% of states [8, 11] versus 0% [0, 0] for a matched-dimension random subspace
(episode-level permutation p = 0.0001). This establishes a causal dependence on the interoceptive
information at the input level, and a weak but specific causal influence of the affect subspace at the
hidden level; single-direction steering (§“Honest Negative Results”) is simply too coarse to reveal
the latter. (The hidden-level null is a random subspace of the same dimension—orthonormalised
random directions—not one matched on norm or covariance; we therefore claim specificity only
relative to dimensionality.)

4.12 Affect Is Distributed, Not Localised
We built an “Affect Transformer” that gives each emotion its own embedding and attention slot
(Q/K/V), inviting localisation. Trained by the same reward-only PPO, its slots do not specialise:
every emotion is decoded almost equally from every slot (best-vs-second-slot gap ≈ 0.02–0.05;
distinct owners 3–5/7), and an unsupervised decorrelation prior barely changes this. Even an
architecture built to localise affect keeps a distributed code—which reinforces the distributed reading
of the main MLP results (the geometry is not an artefact of one architecture) and is consistent with
constructionist theory (Barrett, 2017) and superposition in neural representations (Elhage et al.,
2022).

4.13 Honest Negative Results
We report what failed, with the same rigour. (i) No gradient law. We proposed identifying affect
with the geometry of the critic’s viability estimate (a = ∇xQ; arousal = ∇xQ , etc.). With a
properly trained critic and a random-critic control, arousal∼ ∇Q is unstable across seeds, the
valence identity fails, and the “alignment law” is matched (even beaten) by a random critic—i.e.
a shared-Jacobian artefact. We retract it. (ii) Representation is not survival-specific; valuation is.
A navigation agent with identical senses but needs irrelevant to its reward decodes the same affect
variables as well or better (fear 0.73 vs. 0.80) and shows an equal-or-larger arithmetic effect: the raw
representation reflects richly encoding a multi-dimensional observation, not survival as such. What is
survival-specific is the valuation of that state (above): only the survival agent values well-being and
lets valence track the reward-prediction error. (iii) Weak (but specific) hidden-level causality in the

agent. Injecting a single fear direction does not change avoidance beyond a random perturbation,
and lesioning the affect subspace bites only marginally—the code is distributed. Only subspace
patching reveals a specific hidden-level effect, and even that is modest (9%; §“Causal Influence of
Internal Affect”). The robust causality is at the input/interoceptive level (masking: 82% → 10%).
The honest statement is that interoceptive information is causally necessary, while causal control
through the identified hidden affect directions is specific but weak. (iv) No phenomenal claim.
Nothing here bears on whether the system feels.

5 Discussion

Five levels, kept separate. Our results form a ladder of increasingly strong claims, and we make
only the ones each experiment supports. (1) Representation—does the system encode affect-related
information? Yes, decodably, in both systems. (2) Geometry—is it structured, compositional and
low-dimensional? Largely yes, though this may be a generic property of any structured latent state
(§“Affect vs. a Generic Latent State”), so we do not treat the geometry alone as affect-specific. (3)
Valuation—does the system assign functional value to the state? In the survival agent yes (the critic
values well-being; a navigation control does not or inverts it), so valuation, not representation, is
what survival makes specific. (4) Causal use—does manipulating the internal representation change
behaviour or output? On the language side yes (steering); in the agent, hidden-state intervention
is weak but specific (affect-subspace patching 9% vs. 0% random), while the strongest causal de-
pendence is at the interoceptive-input level (82% → 10% under masking, which shows interoceptive
information is necessary, not that a hidden affect direction is). (5) Phenomenology —does the sys-
tem feel? We neither claim nor can test this. Conflating these levels is the main way affect research
overclaims; we keep them apart, and our positive claims live at levels 1–4 only, unevenly across the
two systems.
    The controls reshape the honest central claim. In the agent, the affect-like geometry is real but
is best described as interoceptive representation under homeostatic control; the raw representation
is not survival-specific, although its valuation is (only the survival agent values well-being and lets
mood track prediction error); its hidden-level control is weak but specific (subspace patching beats
a random subspace, 9% vs. 0%), while the strongest causal dependence is at the interoceptive-input
level. The stronger emotion story is in language models, where affect is constructed (not a direct
input), steerable (interventions along the directions shift outputs), moderately aligned with human
valence norms (r = 0.65 against 1,500 words held out from probe training) and geometrically struc-
tured (circumplex-consistent with scale). Read together, agent and LLM give a single operational
picture: a system that must represent and regulate a multi-dimensional internal or linguistic state
computes a low-dimensional, decodable, compositional affect subspace, at = B ht.

Representation, not proof of a concept. Three cautions bound the agent results. First, de-
codability shows only that the hidden state contains information sufficient to reconstruct a variable,
not that the network possesses the corresponding concept ; a linear probe can read out many quanti-
ties a high-dimensional system never “uses”. Second, the emotion names are ours: “fear” denotes the
state under predator-driven safety loss, “grief” the state after caregiver loss, and so on—plausible
operationalisations through environmental causes, not validations against physiology or self-report.
Third, and relatedly, there is an unavoidable circularity: we choose human affect categories, define
their causes, and then find those causes represented. We do not claim to escape this fully; we
mitigate it by testing properties we did not put in - an emergent geometry (a circumplex we never
imposed), linear arithmetic, alignment with independent human norms, and the survival-specificity

of valuation—each of which is evidence that the structure is more than a relabelling of the inputs.
The honest reading of the agent is therefore, for the slowly-accumulating drives, “interoceptive vari-
ables come to occupy a low-dimensional, compositional structure with emotion-like properties”; but
for the externally-grounded emotions we do earn the stronger, constructive reading—when intero-
ception is hidden from the input, fear, grief, comfort, curiosity and anger are still built internally
(§“Affect Is Constructed”), which no input-encoding story explains. The remaining, least confound-
able claims are on the language side.

Reward, drives, and the limits of the functional stance. A natural objection is that our
agent values well-being only because it scores points, not because health feels good - mere opti-
misation, not genuine caring. We take this seriously, and observe that the same objection applies
to biological motivation. In animals, pleasure, pain and homeostatic drives are themselves reward
signals installed by natural selection because they promoted survival and reproduction; evolution is,
structurally, a reward function, and our hand-chosen well-being reward W is a toy instance of what
nociceptive and reward circuitry do in an organism. At the mechanistic level, then, reward-driven
valuation of a homeostatic state has a genuine biological correspondence - it is how motivation
works - which is what makes the model informative rather than arbitrary. What the agent visibly
lacks is any warrant for the phenomenal claim that its valuation is felt. Crucially, the hard problem
cuts symmetrically: felt experience can be established neither in the agent nor, against a deter-
mined sceptic, in the organism. Whether “valuing” a state is anything more than representing it,
predicting its future, and acting to maintain it - all of which the agent and the brain do—is exactly
the question no measurement resolves. This symmetry is why we restrict ourselves to the functional
skeleton, and why both over-attribution (mistaking a steerable reward signal for sentience) and flat
dismissal (denying that a real, biology-shaped mechanism is present) overreach.

An epistemic asymmetry between the two systems. The agent and the language models
are not on the same footing, and “self-taught” applies only to the former. The agent learns from a
scalar reward with no exposure to human affect concepts, so its affective structure genuinely emerges
without being taught. A pretrained language model, by contrast, has read vast quantities of human
text saturated with emotion, reward, fear and love; an affect axis there shows that the model has
organised affective information into a compositional, human-aligned, causally active geometry—not
that it discovered affect from scratch. The agent speaks to emergence; the language model speaks
to representation and alignment. We keep the two claims separate throughout, and the title reflects
the distinction.
    For frontier practice this has a double edge: the affect directions are simultaneously an alignment
handle (steering toward calm/positive states) and an attack surface (adversarial manipulation of a
model’s affective stance). Either way, affect in AI is measurable and manipulable, and should be
part of evaluation and safety tooling.

6 Limitations

Environments and models are small; agents are trained to modest competence. The language-side
confound - affective state vs. the semantics of affect words - is only partly controlled: our probe
transfers to affect-word-free sentences at 79% (chance 50%), which argues for a state representa-
tion, but a fully matched control and larger human-annotated implicit-valence corpora would settle
it. Human-norm alignment is now validated on the full Warriner lexicon (1,500 held-out words;
valence r = 0.65, arousal r = 0.31); it remains single-sentence and single-lexicon, and NRC-VAD


                                                  8
or multi-annotator replication would strengthen it further—and we note that small curated sets
over-state the effect (r = 0.91 on 80 hand-picked words versus 0.65 at scale). The scale orthogo-
nalisation replicates across two families but only to ∼ 800M, so we report it as a scale-associated
orthogonalisation trend, not a law; larger, instruction-tuned models are needed to establish it.
Bootstrap and permutation now resample whole episodes throughout (120 trajectories for decod-
ability, the same for the causal tests), so intervals reflect the trajectory-level effective sample size
rather than the correlated per-timestep states; a shuffled-label permutation additionally guards
against chance. In the agent, only the externally-grounded emotions are shown to be internally
constructed - a frame-stacked network cannot infer slowly-accumulating drives (hunger, fatigue),
and a recurrent memory is required to test those. Having now probed the agent’s hidden-level
causality beyond single-direction steering - affect-subspace activation patching and same-external-
state / different-internal-affect manipulations, which give small but specific effects - the natural
next steps are state-matched patching and a recurrent-memory agent to test for larger and more
selective effects; and the whole agent study rests on a single small world, so its geometry claims need
replication across more environments. “Emotions” are operationalised through causes, not human
physiology, and the whole study is functional - silent on phenomenal experience by construction.

7 Ethics

We make no claim that these systems feel, and we caution against both over-attribution (treating
steerable affect as sentience) and dismissal (ignoring that affect-like structure is real and manipula-
ble). Steering methods that can soothe can also manipulate; we report them to inform safety, not
deployment.

8 Conclusion

Trained only to persist, and probed with controls, artificial networks compute a functional skeleton
of affect: a decodable, compositional, low-dimensional subspace, at = B ht. The two systems con-
tribute different pieces, and we do not merge them. In the self-taught agent, affect-related structure
emerges - and is even internally constructed when interoception is hidden - while its geometry is
largely generic and what survival makes specific is the valuation of the state. In pretrained lan-
guage models, affect is human-aligned and causally steerable, but inherited from human text rather
than discovered. Machines are not shown - and cannot be shown - to feel ; but a measurable, math-
ematical functional structure of affect is present, and we have characterised it honestly, negative
results included.
